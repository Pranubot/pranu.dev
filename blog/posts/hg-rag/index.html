A discussion about my independent research paper, HG-RAG

07-20-2026

A few months ago I published my first independent research paper on arXiv: HG-RAG: Hierarchy-Guided RAG for Structured Knowledge Graphs. I want to talk about my design decisions and what I learned from doing independent research for the first time.

This spring semester, I got done with my university’s Natural Language Processing class: CSE 188. It was the first time the course had been offered, so there were zero expectations on what it would be like. Despite the ambiguity coming in to the first lecture, I was not disappointed by the course; quite the opposite.

The only assignment to be in the grade book was a final research project related to NLP. No rubric, no guidelines, not even a page limit. This degree of freedom was quite foreign to me, but I immediately saw the opportunity to make this class valuable to me.

When it comes to CS classes, I really favor projects above exams. I don’t say this because it’s low-risk or easy to cheat, but rather their value to your resume. If a course is rigorous enough, the projects you do in them are often sufficient for a place in your resume. As for this course, the project could practically be whatever I wanted to be, with the only constraint being the topic having to be NLP.

A large portion of grading would be allocated to analyzing creativity and originality, so I really wanted to nail down a unique vision. I thought back to an idea my team had for 2025 CalHacks. Our goal was to use node graph maps in conjunction with a RAG retrieval system built for this architecture to understand and build context in virtual game worlds. Recall those map-based grand strategy games with many factions or countries; imagine if we had an AI system that could efficiently retrieve from this map. And not only that, but also edit and build on top of the existing map.

The scope for this project exceeded the manpower we could give in a 2-day period, but it became the basis for my research: Hierarchy-Guided RAG for Structured Knowledge Graphs.

My Setup

I wanted to develop a RAG architecture optimal for operating on node graphs with an emphasis on hierarchical relationships. This style of data shows up frequently in our lives; apart from my map-based video game example where a country contains states who contains cities, we can see this structure in superior-subordinate relationships and any other context where hierarchy and neighboring plays large roles.

Existing RAG operates on semantic similarity through vector embeddings. So relevant entities are encoded into dense vectors and compared to all others using cosine similarity. This system performs well when pulling from flat-text documents; but room for improvement is visible when looking at other data structures, like node-graphs.

You may infer that we can simply translate nodes and edges into flat text relationships, and simply have that as our document which RAG pulls from. This is not a bad solution, as any RAG implementation is better than no RAG; however, you would be losing all advantages of having that specific node structure when flattening it. So my thought process was whether we could implement RAG to analyze a graph the same way humans do: starting from focusing on one node and then shifting attention to neighbors, parents, and children.

With our problem and proposed solution set, it was time to generate a proper testing ground. I decided to play into our video game world example by creating a world which contains complex relationships between different countries and cities. We first start with our planets — they are at the highest tier in our hierarchy, encapsulating the countries who contain the cities. But when we get down to the country level is where things get more interesting. As seen in both media and real-life, countries can often have a allied or adversarial relationship with each other. This behavior is reflected in these procedurally generated worlds I’ve created; countries have information with each other that pertain to relationship, trade, and conflict. Down at the city level, we would track exports/imports between them.

The question I spent a while tackling was “How could I properly evaluate the reliability of HG-RAG as a practical solution?” Multiple kinds of evaluation metrics would have to be developed, as there are a variety of ways you want your data to be retrieved. I landed upon procedurally generating questions the same way I generated the world, but dividing them up into multiple categories, all gauging different ways of understanding the world map.

The first three — local fact, heirarchical, and neighborhood — were quite simple. Local fact would be the most straightforward, essentially asking “Can you retrieve this specific entity I am telling you and tell me the relevant information about them?” Heirarchial evaluated proficiency at retrieving parent/child nodes, while neighborhood focused on lateral nodes. My last query type, multi-hop, addressed an issue I had with the complexity of my trials. I wanted to implement questions that would reward navigating the node graph format — questions that would incentivize exploring the graph as it was presented. Multi-hop was an obvious candidate for this solution, as it forces you chain multiple relational steps across the graph to arrive at the correct answer. It also brings in much needed difficulty to our question pool, easily being the hardest question type.

The issue that arises from multi-hop questions was not generating them. This was accomplished by having a series of template questions which would be selected when applicable to the world. The problem resided in grading them. Grading LLM outputs was uncharted territory for me, so my first instinct was to grade multi-hop responses like any other: keyword checking. This worked well with the former 3 categories, since they had absolute answers. Multi-hop did not have this luxury; there are multiple ways you can word an answer and retain correctness. A simple keyword search would not suffice with this added complexity, so I employed an LLM-as-a-judge. The name is self explanatory: you have a second instance of an LLM to judge the first LLM’s response. This solution initially sounded very hacky to me at first, but apparently its the industry standard when grading LLM responses. I would use a second instance of my LLM and pass it the right context alongside the LLM response to get a score from 1-5 for all multi-hop questions. In a more robust research setting, I would have sported for an API call from one of the industry titans’ large parameter models. However, I did not want to break the bank for a college course project.

Above is a diagram from my paper which illustrates the difference between HG-RAG from a standard vector baseline RAG model. The baseline operates like any standard RAG model, vectorizing every entity and using cosine similarity to find a match. This has its perks when you operate on flat context like a pdf, but when your data takes a less linear form you start to see the struggles. Compare that with HG-RAG, which resolves the entity and traverses the map from the node the same way you would. It’s kind of what I was going for when I started this research: how do we have an LLM observe visual data the same way humans do? When given a map and a question, we have a pretty efficient process to find the answer to that question. We hone our vision on the named entities in the question and go from there, exploring neighbors when necessary.

Results

Now for the results. If you want the specifics on my experimentation, please read my paper, I explain things a lot better there. To summarize, I generated 3 sizes of worlds (small, medium, large) and 100 questions for each world with a 25% split for all query types.

Performing this for multiple trials, the trend is what you expect: HG-RAG performs better as the challenges grew. When world sizes because larger, our baseline RAG buckled under the pressure of massive context to sift through, while HG-RAG’s specialized framework excelled at pinpointing the areas of interest and avoiding unnecessary context buildup. And unsurprisingly, multi-hop questions widened the gap in performance. HG-RAG stayed afloat with respectable performance while our baseline was functionally useless.

However, a result which you may not expect is when observing the small world performance. We actually notice that HG-RAG does not perform better on smaller worlds compared to larger ones. This initially did not make sense to me, because I saw it as a math student being able to do calculus problems but fail at algebra 2. I expected a smaller graph would make retrieval much easier due to less noise, but that’s fails to see the big picture. A smaller graph means that HG-RAG will probably retrieve the entire graph, making the signal-to-noise ratio lopsided and fizzing out any advantage HG-RAG set out to have in the first place. And when the context is so small, there really is no need for some specialized framework with bells and whistles. A standard RAG model can flatten the entire world and experience no struggle since the challenge of traversing a large world that has been flattened is nonexistent.

Overall, the tests were a success, and I was extremely pleased with the solution I came up with. For any data that involves a hierarchical structure which dictates position, relation, or role, an approach along the lines of HG-RAG can prove to be an optimal solution. I’m glad I chose a topic like this to research since it helped conveyed to me the importance of building a framework for specialized tasks. You can always scale up: get better hardware for your AI models. However, the true test lies in how you can maximize the utility through building your own system.

I want to end by thanking my NLP professor Yiwei Wang for endorsing my paper on Arxiv for publishing, I really appreciated his enthusiasm for undergraduate students getting into research. He understood the importance of reading and writing papers in our field, and how overlooked it is in the majority of CS classes. This was a personal favorite class of mine, and I really hope the structure of this course is preserved for future semesters.
