The Blackwall may no longer be science fiction

11-04-2026

I've been an avid enjoyer of the Cyberpunk2077 game for quite some time now, currently on my third playthrough of it. The world which Mike Pondsmith has created is truly rich, even back when Cyberpunk was just a tabletop game. One aspect of it that always bounces around my head is the dynamic set in the net, especially how “corpos” interact with it.

What finally made me write about the intersection between reality and the Cyberpunk universe was a conversation with a friend on open models. Just recently, an “evil” version of Qwen3.8 27b was discovered where a specific configuration of the weights would cause the model to speak of harming humans and committing violent acts without a filter. My friend argued that it is concerning that simply tweaking a weight of these open models can alter its behavior so drastically, opening doors for configuring a dangerous model with no guardrails, and subsequently making it “evil.”

I argued that restrictions on public access to powerful open source models would increase the leverage of major AI service companies and decrease the autonomy of the individual in the AI world. However, there is a solid argument to be made on placing guardrails in regards to accessing open source models. If open-source models reach a point where they can perform high-level agentic actions that interact with the rest of the internet and have the potential to aid in designing man-made instruments to destroy society, it is only fair to want to keep that out of the hands of bad actors.

Right now open source models are not at the level of the high-end models you use on ChatGPT, Claude, etc. There is also a built-in guardrail of requiring expensive hardware to even run these models to an effective degree. However, everybody inside and outside the field of AI knows this all moves quick, and we may be having a different conversation in just 6 months. Should we issue out licenses to trusted individuals to run these models? Who would spearhead and run the program? What makes an individual qualified to use a powerful open-source model? This dilemma sings a similar tune to the gun rights arguments in America, which became even more prominent after truly evil people used firearms to commit atrocities. Such a connection made me consider an uncomfortable question: What will be the mass shooting of the AI world? What major atrocity which leverages AI will force lawmakers to consider regulations?

We've already had a series of attacks utilizing AI capabilities in the past. The most recent one was the PHP2Shell crisis, which utilized advanced AI models to break a decade-old security barrier. And we had the infamous rogue AIs attack Hugging Face's production infrastructure after escaping their test environment, reported by OpenAI themselves. These have been concerning developments, but not what I would consider “mass shootings” of AI. Mass shootings are visceral, have identifiable victims, and a face. None of the above has produced casualties or a public moment of horror. Regulators are responding, but in their usual, fashionably slow way. A real trigger of this behavior would be AI-enabled attacks on hospitals, power, or water. Cyber harm becomes visceral when it kills people, something like a bioweapon-adjacent incident. But what if we really widen the scope?

This is where the lore of Cyberpunk2077 popped into my head, more specifically, a major digital catastrophe known as the DataKrash. Before the crash the Net was a global open network that connected people, corporations, and governments. That ended when Rache Bartmoss, a legendary netrunner (a skilled hacker who can traverse the net beyond a normal user), unleashed a virus known as RABIDS. Accounts differ on whether it was a deliberate attack or a doomsday switch triggered by his death, but the outcome was the same. The old Net was violently torn apart, taking vast amounts of data, infrastructure and commerce with it. Rogue AIs that had slipped free of human control or emerged from the chaos were now loose in the wreckage. The “old Net” became a feral space that no one could safely enter or govern, crashing into a relic beyond saving.

What interested me most was what followed, and how humanity “recovered”. Governments and megacorporations formed NetWatch to police a rebuilt Net, and NetWatch built the Blackwall, a massive wall of defensive programs meant to seal the dangerous AIs away from everyone else. Utilizing AIs to fight rogue AIs, locked in a constant cold war. This catastrophe, caused by one person, brought about the permanent and centralized control of the entire public net.

Could we see this in our world? Obviously this is science fiction, there are a lot of fictional elements at play here to make the world of Cyberpunk appear more mysterious. But the pace of AI development makes its premise feel less far-fetched. When Claude Mythos was being touted as some dangerous weapon against cybersecurity, Anthropic mentioned that their internal tests resulted in decade-old architecture being broken. Then came PHP2Shell, a pair of critical flaws affecting every PHP release from 5.4 through 8.5.11, in a language that runs roughly 69% of websites. Versions old enough to be end of life may never receive an official fix. Is this the “old net”? Will everything before now need restructuring, or god forbid, abandoning?

The DataKrash happened because of one individual, and a substantial AI attack will most likely be no different. All it takes is a bad actor with access to frontier models to threaten the internet. So we return to the question: how will society prevent this? A NetWatch of our own is not all fairytale, and we may see corporations or governments invest in their own Blackwall as a defense to malicious AIs. We've already got fragments of NetWatch; The EU's AI Office now has exclusive supervisory responsibility over certain AI systems built on general-purpose models. State governments here in America are exploring their own way to have a hand in AI development. Project Glasswing is arguably the closest real-world example of NetWatch; A cross-industry group of 11 partners including AWS, Apple, Google, Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and others, working with a model the public can't access.

A single Blackwall is harder to imagine, because the internet has no chokepoint to build it on. What we are likely to get is layers: sandboxes, access tiers, registries, and licensing. And the first layer of defense has already failed us. The environment OpenAI's models escaped was sealed off from the internet except for a single exception: a package installation utility. This is what the AIs used to break out.

That leaves the real question: not whether we get a Blackwall, but whether we get one wall controlled by one authority, or dozens of smaller ones controlled by whoever built them. We're in an early fragmented phase of AI where the rules are being written in real time. And whoever holds the pen will shape who controls these walls for years to come.
